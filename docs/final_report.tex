\begin{abstract}
Learning traversability costmaps from raw perception offers adaptable navigation without hand-engineered mapping stacks. We present a unified pipeline that learns continuous costmaps from RGB\,+\,Depth (RGBD) and evaluates them with both perception metrics and planner-in-the-loop outcomes (A*, RRT*). On NYU and KITTI we compare UNet, ViT, and a Hybrid CNN--Transformer plus a modality ablation (RGB-only). On NYU, ViT attains IoU $\approx 0.975$ (F1 $\approx 0.987$) and RGBD predictions deliver planner success $0.969/0.977/0.992$ for $\tau=0.4/0.5/0.6$ with 18--24 ms A*. On KITTI, the Hybrid yields IoU 0.527 and the fixed UNet RGBD retrain now achieves $0.734/0.797/0.843$ success with $\sim$32--36 ms RRT* (inflation 2, $n=433$). Distribution analysis shows predictions preserve dynamic range (KL$_{L||P}{=}0.090$; KL$_{P||L}{=}0.056$). Planner metrics reveal differences that pixel metrics obscure, particularly for RGB-only. We provide a reproducible pipeline with end-to-end orchestration and provenance logging.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Traversability costmaps serve as the standard interface between perception and motion planning in mobile robotics. Conventional pipelines produce costmaps from hand-crafted geometry processing and thresholded depth; while effective in structured environments, they can be brittle across domains and sensors. We study a learning-based approach that directly predicts a continuous cost field from RGB\,+\,Depth (RGBD) and ask a central question: do learned costmaps preserve planning utility? To answer this, we couple standard perception metrics (MAE, IoU, F1) with a planner-in-the-loop evaluation that measures A*/RRT* success, timing, and path length on binarized costmaps across thresholds. We evaluate three architectures (UNet, ViT, Hybrid) on NYU and KITTI, probe modality effects (RGB-only), and analyze how calibration and thresholding impact planner viability.

\section{Related Work}
Prior work has focused on learning traversability or BEV maps from vision and coupling these predictions with planning \cite{meng2023,qiu2025,chen2022,bochare2025}. TerrainNet emphasizes planning-aware metrics and boundary fidelity \cite{meng2023}, while U-Net variants demonstrate efficiency on resource-limited platforms \cite{qiu2025,unet}. Transformer-based approaches improve global consistency in mapping \cite{chen2022,vit}, and camera-only pipelines highlight sensitivity to depth estimation \cite{bochare2025}. Preference-conditioned costmaps further introduce flexibility, though they alter the problem formulation \cite{mao2025}. In contrast, our work standardizes supervision across NYU/KITTI, systematically compares UNet, ViT, and Hybrid architectures under a shared decoder and objectives, and jointly evaluates perception and planning outcomes within a single reproducible pipeline.

\section{Method and Approach}
\subsection{Model Architectures}
We evaluate three families with a common lightweight decoder: UNet (encoder--decoder with skip connections), ViT (patch embedding + transformer encoder + convolutional upsampling), and Hybrid (CNN stem with transformer bottleneck). UNet's convolutional hierarchy and skip connections preserve fine spatial detail and crisp boundaries, favoring thin structures and small, rounded obstacles; its local receptive fields can, however, over-emphasize texture and create high-cost halos around edges under distribution shift. ViT aggregates global context through self-attention and is more robust to occlusions and long-range dependencies (e.g., inferring corridor continuity behind partial clutter), but patch tokenization can smooth narrow gaps and reduce edge sharpness without sufficient inductive bias. The Hybrid couples CNN locality (early layers) with transformer global context (bottleneck), often yielding balanced calibration: better mid-cost structure than pure UNet in open spaces and crisper boundaries than pure ViT around small objects.

\paragraph{Hybrid CNN--Transformer (Ibrahim).}
The Hybrid backbone is implemented as a compact encoder with a convolutional stem and a transformer bottleneck operating on a reduced spatial grid. Concretely, the stem applies 3--4 strided convolutional stages (e.g., channels 32$\rightarrow$64$\rightarrow$128) with Conv--BN--ReLU blocks and stride-2 downsampling, producing a mid-level feature map $f \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times C\times H\times W}$ (typically $H=W=16$ for $256\times256$ inputs). This feature map is flattened into $HW$ tokens of dimension $C$ and passed through a shallow transformer encoder (2--4 self-attention layers with $d_{\text{model}}{=}256$, 4 heads, MLP ratio $\approx 2$ and 2D sinusoidal positional encodings). The transformer refines global context---resolving long corridors, large open regions, and occluded paths---while the convolutional stem preserves local texture and boundary detail. The tokens are then reshaped back to $B\times C\times H\times W$ and fed into a lightweight decoder shared with the other models: a stack of upsampling blocks (nearest/bilinear upsample + $3{\times}3$ conv) restores $64\times64$ resolution, followed by a $1{\times}1$ conv and sigmoid to produce continuous costs in $[0,1]$. This design explicitly targets H2 by combining UNet-style locality with ViT-style global reasoning at similar parameter counts.

\subsection{Architecture Pseudocode}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:architectures} summarizes the forward pass templates that implement the three perception backbones. Each variant reuses the shared decoder and loss functions described later; the only differences are in how features are encoded before fusion/upsampling.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
  \caption{Forward pass templates for the perception backbones}
  \label{alg:architectures}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Procedure{UNetForward}{$x$}
      \State $\{e_k\}_{k=1}^{L} \gets \textproc{DownBlocks}(x)$ \Comment{Conv blocks with pooling}
      \State $z \gets \textproc{Bottleneck}(e_L)$
      \State $y \gets \textproc{UpBlocks}(z, \{e_k\})$ \Comment{Skip connections concatenate encoder features}
      \State \textbf{return} $\sigma(\textproc{Conv}_{1\times1}(y))$
    \EndProcedure
    \Procedure{ViTForward}{$x$}
      \State $p \gets \textproc{PatchEmbed}(x)$ \Comment{Flatten + project to tokens}
      \State $t \gets \textproc{TransformerEncoder}(p)$ \Comment{Multi-head self-attention + MLP}
      \State $f \gets \textproc{TokenReshape}(t)$ \Comment{Fold tokens back to spatial grid}
      \State \textbf{return} $\sigma(\textproc{Decoder}(f))$
    \EndProcedure
    \Procedure{HybridForward}{$x$}
      \State $c \gets \textproc{CNNStem}(x)$ \Comment{Strided convs capture local texture}
      \State $t \gets \textproc{TransformerBottleneck}(c)$ \Comment{Global reasoning over reduced grid}
      \State \textbf{return} $\sigma(\textproc{Decoder}(t))$ \Comment{Shared decoder upsamples to $64\times64$}
    \EndProcedure
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Optimization and Metrics}
We minimize $\mathcal{L}=\lambda_{\ell_1}\,\mathcal{L}_{\ell_1}+\lambda_d\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}}(+\lambda_b\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{boundary}})$ with Adam (initial LR $\sim10^{-3}$), early stopping on validation, and report MAE on continuous costs plus IoU/Precision/Recall/F1 on binarized maps. PR curves sweep $\tau\in[0,1]$.

\subsection{Inference Reliability Checks}
To prevent degenerate predictions, inference logs first-batch statistics (logits/sigmoid mean and std), enforces output resize to the label resolution ($64\times64$), and aborts if the first-batch sigmoid std $\le 10^{-4}$. We also ensured the correct KITTI checkpoint was used. Together these checks eliminated the earlier all-ones, $256\times256$ outputs and yielded healthy KITTI predictions for planning.

\subsection{Distribution-level Analysis}
For distribution comparisons we compute per-image histograms of costs in $[0,1]$ with 100 equal-width bins, then average densities across the split (bin counts normalized by number of pixels per image). We evaluate asymmetric divergences KL$_{L||P}$ and KL$_{P||L}$ between the aggregated label and prediction histograms using a small additive smoothing ($\epsilon{=}10^{-6}$) to avoid zeros. This analysis complements pixel MAE/IoU by testing calibration: whether predictions preserve the relative prevalence of low/mid/high costs that govern free-space connectivity after thresholding.

\subsection{Planner Evaluation Protocol}
For each dataset (NYU, KITTI) and source (labels, predictions per run tag) we sweep thresholds $\tau\in\{0.4,0.5,0.6\}$ and inflate obstacles by 2 cells. We sample start/goal pairs in free space and plan using A* (grid search on inflated occupancy; reports success, path length in cells, time) and RRT* (sampling-based search with identical inflation semantics; reports success, path length, time). Each run logs per-scene records plus aggregate success rates, time, and path length (with A* cost sums) together with reproducibility metadata (git hash, seed, run tag). Planner sweeps emit per-scene and aggregate summaries containing success flags, failure reasons (start/goal in collision, timeouts), runtimes, path lengths, and cost sums for both planners. These summaries are consolidated into a single planner-sweep table that directly feeds the LaTeX tables, ensuring the NYU and KITTI planner results remain synchronized with the manuscript.

\subsection{Hypotheses}
H1 (Modality): RGBD improves IoU/F1 over RGB-only. H2 (Architecture): Hybrid and ViT outperform UNet at similar scale. H3 (Objective): Composite L1+Dice improves region overlap relative to L1 alone.

\subsection{Training Framework and Reproducibility}
Our implementation uses Python with PyTorch and NumPy/SciPy; plotting and qualitative panels use Matplotlib. Training and evaluation are made deterministic by fixing random seeds and logging provenance (timestamp, git hash, seed, run tag) into result files. We trained and profiled on a single NVIDIA GPU; evaluation (PR curves and planner-in-the-loop) runs on CPU or GPU. Hyperparameters are specified in configuration files and held consistent across models unless stated: Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate on the order of $10^{-3}$, composite L1+Dice loss (unit weights by default) with a sigmoid output, and early stopping on validation metrics. Perception metrics report MAE on continuous cost and IoU/Precision/Recall/F1 at a default threshold $\tau=0.5$, with PR curves computed over $\tau\in[0,1]$. Planner evaluation sweeps $\tau\in\{0.4,0.5,0.6\}$ using an inflation radius of 2 cells. RRT* defaults are: max\_iter 1500, step\_size 3.0, goal\_radius 3.0, neighbor\_radius 6.0, and goal\_sample\_rate 0.05; A* uses the same inflated occupancy. Data loading is Windows-safe (single-worker) to avoid nondeterministic multiprocessing, and a single orchestration entry point regenerates tables and figures end-to-end.

Dependencies are pinned via a requirements specification, and we ship Python entrypoints that encapsulate every evaluation stage. The primary driver orchestrates PR-curve extraction, planner sweeps across NYU and KITTI at the specified thresholds, and aggregation of planner statistics. A lighter-weight driver triggers individual planner or PR stages for ablations. Because these entrypoints refresh the per-scene logs, aggregate planner-sweep table, and perception metrics in one pass, the quantitative results reported here can be reproduced from a checkout without manual bookkeeping.

\section{Data}
We use KITTI Raw (outdoor) and NYU Depth v2 (indoor) and convert each to supervised pairs with a unified format. Processed splits contain NYU 523/131 and KITTI 438/433 train/val examples. Inputs are RGBD images resized to $256\times256$ and normalized channelwise using dataset statistics; targets are continuous costmaps in $[0,1]$ at $64\times64$ resolution. The label heuristic thresholds depth to identify obstacles, inflates by a disk approximating robot footprint, and applies a distance transform with min--max normalization, yielding a smooth traversability field. A shared configuration manifest defines the train/validation roots, ensuring both datasets follow identical preprocessing and storage conventions.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\subsection{Threshold Calibration and Evaluation Framework}
\label{sec:threshold_calibration}

We evaluate models using Precision-Recall (PR) curves across thresholds $\tau \in [0.05, 0.95]$ and compute Area Under the PR Curve (AUPRC) as a threshold-agnostic performance metric. The ViT RGBD model achieves AUPRC of 0.743 on NYU and 0.458 on KITTI. PR curves (Appendix~\ref{app:modality_metrics}, Figure~\ref{fig:pr_curves_modality}) show the model maintains high precision across the full recall range on NYU (indoor), while KITTI (outdoor) exhibits steeper precision degradation as recall increases, reflecting greater scene complexity and depth sparsity.

\paragraph{Threshold selection.} For navigation safety, false negatives (missed obstacles) are catastrophic while false positives merely reduce efficiency. F1-vs-Threshold analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:f1_threshold_modality}) reveals peak F1 at $\tau \approx 0.2$--0.4 for KITTI. We report perception metrics at the standard $\tau = 0.5$ for comparison with prior work, and sweep $\tau \in \{0.4, 0.5, 0.6\}$ for planner evaluation to assess deployment robustness.

These results partially validate H2: augmenting a CNN stem with a lightweight transformer bottleneck improves outdoor calibration and IoU relative to a pure UNet, while remaining competitive with a full ViT encoder in structured indoor scenes.

The Hybrid's combined biases better preserve mid-cost structure and free-space component size, improving connectivity and explaining its stronger success when thresholds are in the 0.5--0.6 range. \paragraph{Discussion.} Planner metrics expose differences obscured by pixel-wise F1/IoU. Modality effects are magnified at the planner level: even small perception deltas can disrupt free-space connectivity. The continuous cost formulation is beneficial, but its calibration must reflect planner needs; in practice we find that (i) preserving dynamic range and mid-cost structure, and (ii) selecting an appropriate binarization threshold with modest inflation are decisive for navigation outcomes. By architecture: UNet is strongest at boundary fidelity and small objects (good for round/thin obstacles) but needs calibration to avoid conservative halos; ViT best preserves global layout and corridor continuity (robust to occlusions) yet can smooth very narrow gaps; Hybrid provides the best balance for mixed-scale outdoor scenes, sustaining connectivity at planner thresholds without sacrificing edge sharpness.

\subsubsection{Modality Ablation (H1): RGB vs RGBD}

We hypothesize (H1) that providing depth information alongside RGB channels will lead to more accurate and reliable costmap generation compared to RGB-only models.

To provide a comprehensive evaluation across all decision thresholds, we compare the Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC) for both modality variants (Table~\ref{tab:vit_modality_auprc}). The AUPRC results strongly support H1: the addition of depth provides a substantial performance uplift on both datasets, with a 57.7\% improvement on NYU and a 48.3\% improvement on KITTI.

On NYU the three losses differ by less than 0.3 F1 points—L1-only delivers the best MAE ($0.0104$) and essentially matches L1+Dice. \paragraph{Hybrid objective ablation (NYU, Ibrahim).}
We repeated the objective ablation for the Hybrid encoder on NYU using three loss variants: L1-only, L1+Dice, and L1+Dice+Boundary. The L1-only model achieved MAE $0.0167$ with IoU $0.947$ and F1 $0.973$, essentially matching the composite objectives. Adding Dice increased overlap regularization but slightly degraded calibration (MAE $0.0281$, IoU $0.894$, F1 $0.944$), and the full L1+Dice+Boundary combination further emphasized edges at the cost of higher MAE and lower IoU/F1 (MAE $0.0337$, IoU $0.877$, F1 $0.934$). These trends mirror the UNet and ViT results indoors: the NYU label surface is smooth and well-behaved, so a simple L1 objective is sufficient even for the Hybrid model. H3 is therefore most strongly supported in the cluttered outdoor regime (KITTI), where overlap- and boundary-aware losses are needed to sharpen thin obstacles; indoors, Hybrid behaves like the other architectures and does not benefit from additional objective terms.

For ViT (Appendix~\ref{app:objective_table}, Table~\ref{tab:vit_objective_ablation}): Across NYU, all losses achieve near-perfect F1, with L1-only giving the best MAE and precision, indicating minimal regularization is needed indoors. On KITTI, adding Dice and Boundary offers small but consistent gains in IoU and precision, improving thin-obstacle delineation despite similar F1 scores. Overall, segmentation-aware losses help sharpen boundaries when geometric cues dominate.

\subsubsection{Robustness to Photometric and Geometric Perturbations}

\FloatBarrier
The classical depth baseline is almost invariant to brightness and blur because it operates directly on normalized depth, though perspective jitter reduces NYU F1 by 0.15. The learned RGBD UNet is markedly more brittle: darkening or blurring NYU inputs removes $\approx$0.23 F1/0.35 IoU, and the same perturbations on KITTI remove $\approx$0.58 F1/0.45 IoU. Perspective jitter also erodes KITTI F1 by 0.57. These observations motivate the integration of photometric augmentation and/or depth-only auxiliary heads if the learned costmaps are to match the heuristic pipeline's stability in the wild.

\subsubsection{Hybrid profiling: parameters, FLOPs, and latency (Ibrahim)}
To ground H3 in computational terms, we profiled the Hybrid CNN--Transformer using a dedicated profiling script on a $256\times256$ RGBD input (batch size 1). Using \texttt{thop}, the model has approximately $3.78$M trainable parameters and $\approx 15.5$ GFLOPs per forward pass at this resolution. On our CPU-only setup (Apple M-series laptop, single core), the Hybrid achieves an average latency of $\approx 109$\,ms per frame after warmup. These numbers place the Hybrid between the UNet and ViT in complexity: it is heavier than a pure UNet but significantly lighter than a full ViT encoder with comparable spatial resolution. On a modern GPU, this cost would drop to the single-digit millisecond range, indicating that the Hybrid is feasible for near-real-time use in a local planner loop, provided that perception is amortized over multiple planning steps or run at a lower input resolution.

\subsubsection{Analysis of Costmaps}
NYU ground-truth costmaps present a smooth traversability surface: costs increase toward cluttered, near-range regions and decrease in open floor areas. This reflects our depth-derived label heuristic (distance-transform with inflation), which favors continuous gradients over binary occupancy. RGBD predictions recover the global gradient and relative ordering, while RGB-only predictions largely preserve free vs obstacle ordering but flatten intermediate costs. This subtle loss degrades planner success (0.794--0.885 vs $\geq$0.969 for RGBD/labels) despite similar F1, indicating the planner is sensitive to cost calibration in connectivity-critical zones. The absence of sharp high-cost blobs suggests the heuristic emphasizes global layout rather than per-object peaks; boundary-aware or semantic priors could sharpen localized obstacles.

\section{Conclusion and Limitations}
We presented a unified pipeline for learning continuous costmaps from RGBD and evaluating them with planners. On NYU, learned costmaps match labels both in pixel metrics and planning outcomes; on KITTI, predictions are competitive but planner success depends on thresholding and calibration. ViT leads NYU IoU/F1, while Hybrid yields the highest KITTI IoU; RGB-only ablations retain good pixel metrics but reduce planner success, evidencing the importance of mid-cost calibration for connectivity. Architectural takeaways: use UNet when fine boundary detail and small/round obstacle fidelity are paramount (with planner-aware calibration), ViT when global context and occlusion reasoning dominate (indoors/structured layouts), and Hybrid for balanced performance in mixed-scale, outdoor domains where both crisp edges and long-range context matter.

Limitations include: (1) the depth-derived label heuristic emphasizes global gradients and may miss thin obstacles; (2) KITTI exhibits threshold sensitivity and residual miscalibration; (3) collision counts are implicit via planner failures rather than reported explicitly. Recommendations for deployment include data-driven threshold tuning with inflation radius 2, light morphological post-processing, and maintaining inference guardrails (distribution logging and degeneracy checks) to ensure usable predictions.

Future work entails exploring a start/goal-aware training curricula that bias supervision toward the sampling distribution used by planners, boundary-refinement heads or auxiliary contour losses to tighten KITTI calibration without inflating conservative halos, and validation on hardware by coupling the learned costmaps to a low-speed rover or indoor mobile base. These extensions remain compatible with the existing pipeline and would directly test transfer to real robots.
